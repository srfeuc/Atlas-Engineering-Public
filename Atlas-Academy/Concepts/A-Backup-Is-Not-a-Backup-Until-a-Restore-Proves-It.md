## The Concept

Backup and recovery are haunted by one confusion: **a backup is a claim, not a fact, until a restore has succeeded.** A file that *should* restore, an archive that encrypted without error, a backup job that reported green — these are all `gpg exited 0` in a different costume. The evidence is the restore: the archive opens, the key checks, the VM boots. Everything before that is a hope with good intentions.

Three ideas follow from it.

**3-2-1, and the "1" is the whole point.** Three copies, on two kinds of media, with **one off-site**. A second copy on the same host — or a `.gpg` on an `E:` drive in the same room — is *redundancy*, and redundancy dies with the room. The off-site copy is the only one that survives the event backups exist for (fire, theft, flood). "We have backups" and "we can recover from losing the building" are different claims, and only the off-site copy bridges them.

**Recovery objectives are written and measured, and where the asset is event-driven, so is the objective.** Every protected asset gets a stated **RPO** (how much you can afford to lose) and **RTO** (how long you can be down), and both are *measured in a drill*, not asserted. For most data RPO is a clock (last night's backup). For a certificate authority it's an **event**: back up immediately after any issue/revoke/rotate, because a certificate issued *after* the last backup restores as an orphan the CA has no record of and can't revoke — a stopwatch can't protect you from that, only "back up on the event" can.

**Continuity is not recovery.** *Continuity* keeps a function available *during* a disruption (redundancy, failover, a manual fallback); *recovery* restores it *after* it's gone. A home lab full of single points of failure has little true continuity, and the honest move is to say so: Atlas's continuity story is mostly **graceful degradation + fast, proven recovery**, plus naming the manual fallback (take orders on paper) rather than pretending a single firewall is resilient. The traps that quietly break continuity are dependency loops — the vault that holds every credential running on the host you're about to wipe, "out-of-band" management that shares the uplink it's supposed to survive — and each one is broken by an *independent* path (a paper passphrase, a real console).

**And the test has to be allowed to fail.** The ultimate backup test isn't restoring the file — it's **rebuilding from the documentation alone**: no chat log, no memory, no "I remember I set that." A rehearsal you run on a working system with the docs open in front of you can't fail in the way that matters; a drill whose target and timing *you* pick is scoped to what you already know works. The value is in being handed a failure you didn't choose — because that's the only condition under which the *gaps* get selected instead of the *strengths*. **A drill that cannot fail is a ceremony.**

## The Atlas Example (real artifacts)

**The one backup Atlas has actually proven ([`049`](../../Labs/Lab-01-Mikrotik-Core/Operations/049-Root-CA-and-Credential-Backup-Runbook.md)).** The Root-CA credential backup is the estate's richest verified transcript — every command run on the live device with its real output. The line that defines the whole discipline is its restore proof: `openssl rsa -in restore-test/…/root-ca.key -noout -check` → `RSA key ok` — *"this is the moment the file becomes a backup."* And its **pre-archive `ls`** *"found the most serious problem of the night"* — two `…key.bak` copies wrapped in the old, exposed passphrase, never destroyed because the procedure had no destroy step (the [Secrets & Credential Custody](./Secrets-and-Credential-Custody.md) lesson, and `POL-0005`'s destroy-step rule).

**The rules, on real targets (`POL-0005` / `POL-0013`).** Tiered protected assets (Tier 0 = the CA keys + issuance DB, unrebuildable; Tier 1 = the vault, device configs, AD state; Tier 2 = rebuildable service data); **3-2-1** with a named off-site copy; a **restore test per backup class** on a cadence; a written **RPO/RTO** per asset — owned by [`CA-PKI-Recovery-Objectives-RPO-RTO`](../../Labs/Lab-02-Cisco-Core/Architecture/CA-PKI-Recovery-Objectives-RPO-RTO.md), which sets the CA RPO to **zero un-backed-up mutating events** and the site-loss RTO to **∞ until the off-site copy exists**; and a hard **pre-teardown gate** ([`Pre-Teardown-Backup-and-Verify-Checklist`](../../Labs/Lab-02-Cisco-Core/Architecture/Pre-Teardown-Backup-and-Verify-Checklist.md)) — *"do not wipe a device until its irreplaceable data is backed up, restore-verified, and copied to a second location."* The device roster (BKP01 = PBS + Vaultwarden, 📋 not built) and the full record set live in the [Backup directory](../Directory/Backup-Recovery-and-Continuity.md).

**The honest gap, marked not hidden (`POL-0001`/`POL-0006`).** The estate says out loud that its two biggest real risks are here: **the restore Game Day has never run in Atlas**, and **the encrypted off-site copy does not yet exist** (both archive copies sit in one room). This whole domain is rich in *design* and thin in *proof* — which is exactly the state `POL-0005` exists to make auditable rather than comfortable.

## What Went Wrong (real troubleshooting history — the best teacher)

The discipline is a distillation of the estate's frozen-Lab-01 recovery seam (`ADR-0022`).

- **Rehearsal vs. test — the Game-Day case ([`ADR-0011`](../../00-Atlas-Foundation/Decisions/ADR-0011-Game-Day-Unannounced-Failure-Drills.md)).** Atlas's central claim is "the documentation is good enough to rebuild from," and it *had never been tested* — `048` describes a rebuild and `049` proved the backup, but both were run by their author, on a working system, with the docs open: *"a rehearsal, not a test. It cannot fail in the way that matters."* The drill's third move — rebuild from documentation *alone* — is the only one that can falsify the project's claim, and the target must be declared by someone else because *"a drill you design is a drill you unconsciously scope to what you know you can do."* The ADR lists **five real defects** (FreeRADIUS unauthenticatable for a day, MKT01's RADIUS never completed, Pi01's firewall entirely inactive, the CA's no-SAN defect, the committed passphrase) — *"every one found by accident; a drill would have found them on purpose."*
- **The circular dependency ([`048`](../../Labs/Lab-01-Mikrotik-Core/Operations/048-Teardown-and-Rebuild-Runbook.md)).** *"Vaultwarden runs on Pi01. Every device password is stored in Vaultwarden. Wipe Pi01 and you have deleted your own credentials."* The trap is a backup plan whose recovery depends on the thing being recovered; it's broken only by extracting credentials *before* the wipe and by a **paper** passphrase that survives the host. (Lab-02 moves the vault off the wiped host to BKP01 to close it.)
- **The integrity control that was 40% blind ([`CM-0032`](../../Labs/Lab-01-Mikrotik-Core/Change-Management/CM-0032-CA-Database-Has-No-Record-of-Two-Certificates.md)).** The CA's `index.txt` held four rows while the CA had signed six trusted certs — two issued a way that never writes the DB. A backup that faithfully captures a *wrong* baseline restores a control that *"fails loud and wrong,"* which is why the CA's RPO is event-based: back up after every issuance, or the DB and reality drift.
- **The fix that reached every doc except the runbook ([`CM-0025`](../../Labs/Lab-01-Mikrotik-Core/Change-Management/CM-0025-048-Phase-0-Rebuilds-the-Destroyed-Archive.md)).** `CM-0010`'s correction updated everything that *describes* the CA backup but not the runbook that *takes* one, so `048` Phase 0 would have rebuilt the exact archive `CM-0010` had just destroyed. A recovery procedure is only as good as the last time someone reconciled it against the change that invalidated it.
- **Never destroy the rollback before the replacement verifies ([`CM-0010`](../../Labs/Lab-01-Mikrotik-Core/Change-Management/CM-0010-CA-Passphrase-Rotation-and-Exposed-Key-Destruction.md)).** The old tarball was `shred`'d only *after* the new backup proved out — because while the old archive is the only thing that opens, it's the only recovery point that exists. The read-back rule the whole thing is measured against comes from [`015`](../../Labs/Lab-01-Mikrotik-Core/Operations/015-Network-Validation-Guide.md): *"read the resulting state off the device. Not the exit code. Not the absence of an error. The value."*

## How to Explain This in an Interview

*"My one rule for backups is that a backup is a claim until a restore turns it into evidence — a job that reports success or an archive that encrypted cleanly proves nothing; the proof is opening it, checking the key, booting the VM. In my lab the only backup I fully trust is the one where I restored the CA and ran `openssl rsa -check` and got `RSA key ok` — that's the moment the file became a backup. On top of that: 3-2-1, where the copy that matters is the off-site one, because two copies in the same room both die in the same fire; and RPO/RTO written down and actually measured in a drill, event-driven for something like a CA where a cert issued after the last backup comes back as an orphan you can't revoke. I also separate continuity from recovery — continuity is staying up during the failure, recovery is coming back after — and I'm honest that a lab full of single points of failure mostly has graceful degradation plus fast recovery, not real HA. The sharpest lesson is about testing: the real test isn't restoring a file, it's rebuilding from the documentation alone, with no chat log and no memory, on a target someone else picked — because a drill you design is scoped to what you already know works, and a drill that can't fail is just a ceremony."*
