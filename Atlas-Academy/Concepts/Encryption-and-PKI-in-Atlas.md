# Encryption & PKI in Atlas (one module)

<!-- provenance -->
> **Atlas Academy — Concepts.** A "why it works" module (`Academy/README` 4-part format). Every claim points at a **real Atlas artifact** — a named cipher on a named box, a real cert incident, a real key-custody scar. The *settings* live in [`STD-0004` Encryption](../../00-Atlas-Foundation/Standards/STD-0004-Encryption.md) and the *roster* in the [Identity & Access directory](../Directory/Identity-and-Access.md); this page explains the **reasoning** so the crypto is understood, not just copied. One home per fact (`POL-0004`) — where a value or a record lives elsewhere, this page links it.

> **The gap this closes ([`STD-0004` Learn-it](../../00-Atlas-Foundation/Standards/STD-0004-Encryption.md)):** the encryption standard names strong values on real devices — CTR-only SSH, a DH-2048 floor, a documented SHA1-MAC ceiling, estate-CA certs never self-signed, offline-vaulted CA keys — but a standard says *what*, not *why*. `STD-0004` flagged that a dedicated Encryption-and-PKI concept was **warranted** as its why-layer. This is it: why a two-tier CA, why a SAN and a working CRL are non-negotiable, why scrypt over Type-7, and why one deliberate deviation from Microsoft's sample is the *more* secure choice here.

## The Concept

Encryption in an estate is two problems that people blur together. **Confidentiality on the wire and at rest** — is the traffic and the disk unreadable to someone who taps or steals it — and **trust** — when a device presents a certificate, can a relying party *prove* it's talking to the real thing. Strong ciphers solve the first; a **PKI** (public-key infrastructure) solves the second, and the second is where estates actually get burned.

**The transport/at-rest half.** "Encrypted" is not a yes/no; it's *which algorithm, at what strength*. A management plane that offers an old CBC cipher, 3DES, or a 768-bit Diffie-Hellman group is negotiable *down* to that weak option by anyone in the path — the strong ciphers you also offer don't help. So the rule is **remove the weak options**, not just add strong ones: SSH v2 only, CTR ciphers only, a DH modulus floor, no cleartext management protocol at all. Secrets stored on the box get a **memory-hard hash** (scrypt), not a reversible cipher — a stored password you can decrypt is a stored password an attacker can decrypt. Disks and backups get named algorithms (AES-256), because "it's encrypted" with no algorithm named is folklore.

**The trust half — why *two* tiers.** A certificate authority is only as trustworthy as its private key. If that key lives on an always-on, internet-adjacent box, one compromise forges trust for the entire estate and there is no clean recovery — you'd have to re-establish the trust anchor on every device. The **two-tier** design fixes this: a **Root CA that is powered off** (air-gapped) signs exactly one thing — the issuing CA's certificate — then goes dark; a separate **online Issuing CA** does the day-to-day minting. Compromise the online issuer and you rebuild *it* without touching the root, so nothing re-imports trust. The root's private key, the thing whose exposure is unrecoverable, is offline where it can't be reached.

**Why trust fails in practice — three details that aren't optional.** A PKI breaks not on the math but on the plumbing: (1) a certificate needs a correct **SAN** (Subject Alternative Name) or modern clients reject it regardless of a valid signature; (2) it needs a reachable **revocation path** (a CRL distribution point served over HTTP) or a revoked certificate stays trusted forever — *"a revocation nobody checks is a filing action, not a security control"* (`ADR-0009`); and (3) the signature algorithm must be one **every** relying party can validate, or a device that can't validate it pressures an operator into turning validation *off*, which is worse than a weaker-but-universal algorithm. Atlas learned all three at the machine, the hard way.

## The Atlas Example (real artifacts)

**Strong transport, provable — the device-verified half (`STD-0004` R1–R2).** SW01 and the 1941 present SSH **v2 only** with **CTR ciphers only** (`aes256-ctr, aes192-ctr, aes128-ctr` — CBC and 3DES removed), a **DH modulus floor of 2048** (`ip ssh dh min size 2048`), no telnet, and no `ip http server` — read back with `show ip ssh` and captured in [`CIS-Hardening-SW01`](../../Labs/Lab-02-Cisco-Core/Architecture/CIS-Hardening-SW01.md) / [`CIS-Hardening-1941`](../../Labs/Lab-02-Cisco-Core/Architecture/CIS-Hardening-1941.md). IOS secrets are **Type 9 (scrypt)** — a memory-hard hash — never Type 7 (trivially reversible) or Type 5 (MD5); the working password lives in Vaultwarden, never in the config (`POL-0002`). These two are ✅ **device-verified (2026-07-22)** — the read-back exists.

**The one honest ceiling (`STD-0004` R1a).** On the 1941 ISR G2 and the Catalyst 2960X, the IOS image offers **`hmac-sha1` MACs only** — no SHA2 — and the key exchange can't be pinned. That's a *hardware/image* limit, not a config gap: SHA2 MACs need a newer image the hardware won't run. The mitigation is real (`ip ssh dh min size 2048` floors the modulus so the weak 768-bit `group1` can't negotiate) and the deviation is **documented, not silent** — the difference between an accepted risk and a forgotten one.

**The trust anchor — the two-tier AD CS PKI ([`ADR-0027`](../../00-Atlas-Foundation/Decisions/ADR-0027-AD-CS-Two-Tier-Microsoft-PKI.md)).** [`RCA01`](../../Labs/Lab-02-Cisco-Core/Devices/RCA01-ICA01-ADCS/) is a workgroup, **offline** standalone Root CA — RSA-4096 / SHA-256, ~20-year validity — that signs `ICA01` once and powers off. [`ICA01`](../../Labs/Lab-02-Cisco-Core/Devices/RCA01-ICA01-ADCS/) is a domain-joined **Enterprise Issuing CA** (not co-located on a DC — blast-radius separation) that mints every estate cert: DC LDAPS, NPS PEAP, service TLS. Both are **Tier 0** (`ADR-0021`), administered only from the PAW, and templates are hardened against the ESC1–ESC8 escalation paths from the start. The build is faithful to Microsoft's current recommended two-tier design (audited against the Test Lab Guide + the PKI-design page) with **one deliberate deviation**: `AlternateSignatureAlgorithm=0`. Microsoft's sample uses `1` (RSASSA-PSS), but PSS is validated unreliably by the estate's non-Windows relying parties — the FortiGate (`ADR-0028`), MikroTik — so PKCS#1 v1.5 is chosen precisely because it's *universally* interoperable. A certificate a device can't validate is a security failure, so the interoperable choice is the more secure one here.

**Working revocation, by design (`STD-0004` R3/R5).** Every issued cert carries a **CRL Distribution Point** and an **AIA** URL served over HTTP (`pki.atlas.lab`), reachable *before* any leaf is issued; the acceptance gate is `pkiview.msc` all-green **plus an observed, honoured revocation** — revocation proven, not assumed. The issuance database (`index.txt`) is treated as an **integrity control** (`ADR-0009`): its rows must match the certs actually deployed, because it is the only way to detect an unauthorised issuance. The R3/R5 controls are **📋 gated** on the AD CS root ceremony (the tallest unbuilt dependency) — honestly marked, no ✅ until a real issued cert is read back off the wire.

**One PKI, not two ([`ADR-0031`](../../00-Atlas-Foundation/Decisions/ADR-0031-Retire-OpenSSL-Lab-CA.md)).** Atlas originally ran a separate OpenSSL Lab CA for the non-domain gear (`ADR-0003` coexistence). That was reversed: the non-domain devices fold onto AD CS (reissued from ICA01, trusting the RCA01 root), the OpenSSL CA is retired, and there is one trust anchor, one enrollment story, one CRL to keep alive — but **migrate-and-test first** (`ADR-0031` D5): no device's old trust is torn out until its AD CS replacement is proven and the revocation gate passes.

**Encrypted at rest (`STD-0004` R4).** BitLocker on Windows clients and the PAW, LUKS on the offline CA media, AES-256 on backup archives (`gpg --cipher-algo AES256`), the Vaultwarden DB encrypted — named algorithms on named targets, 📋 for the hosts not yet built.

For the full device roster and the frozen record index, see the [Identity & Access directory §3/§5](../Directory/Identity-and-Access.md#3-the-two-tier-pki-trust-chain); this module is the *why* behind it.

## What Went Wrong (real troubleshooting history — the best teacher)

The estate's PKI discipline was paid for in real incidents, all frozen in Lab-01 (`ADR-0022`; the OpenSSL CA they name is retired, `ADR-0031` — read them for *how the discipline was learned*).

- **A clean sign log, an empty SAN ([`MC-0002`](../../Labs/Lab-01-Mikrotik-Core/Devices/MKT01-Core-Router/Changes/MC-0002-MikroTik-Certificate-Reissuance-and-CA-Fix.md)).** A one-line SAN correction uncovered that the CA had been missing `copy_extensions` **since it was built** — so `openssl ca` signed a certificate cleanly, with no error, that had *no SAN at all*. It was caught only because the engineer read the actual file (`openssl x509 -text | grep SAN`) instead of trusting the sign log. This is the incident the Academy's golden Playbook is cut from, and it's why `STD-0004` R3 names "a correct SAN" as a hard requirement, not a nicety.
- **A perfect install of the factory certificate ([`MC-0001`](../../Labs/Lab-01-Mikrotik-Core/Devices/FGT01-NS-Firewall/Changes/MC-0001-FGT01-Lab-CA-Certificate-Installation.md)).** A "20-minute" FortiGate cert install became a four-layer diagnostic. Every certificate-side step was done right on the first try; the actual defect was that `set admin-server-cert` **silently never took effect** — the GUI served the factory cert the whole time. `show` hid it (it only prints non-defaults); only `get system global | grep admin-server-cert` surfaced the truth. Two more traps stacked underneath: a `CA Certificate` import is *not* the same as bundling the intermediate into what the device presents, and Chrome's TLS session cache outlived a correct server-side fix. The lesson — *a successful-looking command is not a confirmed change; read the live state back* — became the [`A-Completed-Command-Is-Not-Evidence`](./A-Completed-Command-Is-Not-Evidence.md) concept and the `STD-0004` "verify on the wire, not in the file" rule.
- **The stale-SAN reissue ([`CM-0007`](../../Labs/Lab-01-Mikrotik-Core/Devices/MKT01-Core-Router/Changes/CM-0007-Install-Lab-CA-Certificate-on-MikroTik.md) → [`CM-0008`](../../Labs/Lab-01-Mikrotik-Core/Devices/MKT01-Core-Router/Changes/CM-0008-Reissue-MikroTik-Certificate-Correct-SAN.md)).** A *perfect install of the wrong certificate*: the import succeeded, but the SAN was a pre-VLAN address (`10.0.0.1`), so browsers rejected it. A clean install and a correct certificate are different things.
- **Revocation that reached nothing ([`ADR-0009`](../../00-Atlas-Foundation/Decisions/ADR-0009-Intermediate-CA-Not-Treated-as-Compromised.md)).** When `MC-0002` revoked the broken cert (serial `1000`), the revocation reached **no client** — the CA issued nothing with a `crlDistributionPoints` extension and served no CRL over HTTP, so no client was ever told where to look. That single fact is why `ADR-0027` makes a reachable CDP a *hard gate* on the new PKI, and why R5 makes `index.txt` a control.
- **Key and passphrase on one box for fifteen hours ([`ADR-0009`](../../00-Atlas-Foundation/Decisions/ADR-0009-Intermediate-CA-Not-Treated-as-Compromised.md) / [`CM-0010`](../../Labs/Lab-01-Mikrotik-Core/Change-Management/CM-0010-CA-Passphrase-Rotation-and-Exposed-Key-Destruction.md)).** A backup procedure created `.bak` copies of the CA keys **wrapped in the old passphrase** and had *no destroy step*, so key material and the passphrase that opens it sat on the same workstation for ~15 hours. The remedy wasn't "be more careful" — it was a rule: **rotate a leaked passphrase before any backup, never the reverse**, and every backup procedure carries a destroy step. That is `STD-0004` R5's "separate passphrases, vaulted, rotation-precedes-backup."
- **The passphrase in the commit ([`CM-0014`](../../Labs/Lab-01-Mikrotik-Core/Change-Management/CM-0014-Archive-Passphrase-Committed-to-Repository.md)).** The defining secrets scar — a backup passphrase committed *in the same commit that shipped the runbook forbidding it*; a bare high-entropy string has no shape, so a content scanner said "no leaks found" and only the **filename** gave it away. It's why the estate never `git add .`'s and scans by filename. *(No secret value is reproduced here — `POL-0002`.)*

## How to Explain This in an Interview

*"I think about encryption as two jobs. On the wire, 'encrypted' isn't a yes/no — it's which algorithm at what strength, and the trick is removing the weak options, not just adding strong ones, because a session negotiates down to the weakest thing you still offer. So on my switches I stripped SSH to v2 with CTR ciphers only, floored Diffie-Hellman at 2048, killed telnet and HTTP, and stored device secrets as scrypt — and where the hardware could only do SHA1 MACs, I documented that as an accepted ceiling with a mitigation, rather than pretending it wasn't there. The second job is trust, and that's a PKI. I built a two-tier CA the Microsoft way: an offline root that's powered off and signs the issuing CA once, and an online issuing CA that does the day-to-day certs — so if the online one is ever compromised I rebuild it without re-establishing trust anywhere. The details that actually bite are the boring ones: certificates need a correct SAN or clients reject them, they need a reachable CRL or revocation is theatre, and the signature algorithm has to be one every device — including my FortiGate and MikroTik — can actually validate, which is why I deliberately chose PKCS#1 v1.5 over Microsoft's PSS default. I learned every one of those the hard way — I once signed a certificate cleanly that had no SAN at all, and caught it only because I read the file back instead of trusting the sign log."*
